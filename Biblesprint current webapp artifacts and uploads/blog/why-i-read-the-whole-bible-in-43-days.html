Why I read the whole Bible in 43 days

Most of us have started at Genesis 1 — and stalled somewhere in Leviticus. Or we've read the same favourite passages so many times we could quote them, but we've never seen the whole story. The Bible has 1,189 chapters. Most people who own one have read maybe two hundred.

For years, that was me. Until something shifted.

The breakthrough

A few years ago I was praying about how to grow deeper in the Word. Not just read more, but understand more — see the connections, hear the rhythm, watch the same God show up in Genesis and Revelation. The answer the Lord gave me was almost too simple: read in sprints of seven chapters. Just seven. Then stop. Then come back tomorrow and do another seven.

I tried it. The first day I read Matthew 1–7 in a single sitting. The next day, Matthew 8–14. By the time I'd finished the New Testament, eleven days in, I felt something I'd never felt reading the Bible before: I knew what I'd just read. The story wasn't broken into Sunday-morning fragments anymore. It was a whole.

By Day 43 I'd read every chapter of the Bible. From Matthew to Malachi, gospels to prophets, no gaps, no skipping. And I started again.

Why sprints work

Reading seven chapters at a time forces you to read for the story, not for the sermon. You can't slow down and parse every Hebrew root — you'd never finish. So you read the way you'd read a letter from a friend: at speed, for the meaning, for the voice.

Three things changed for me when I started reading like this:

  1. Patterns appeared. The same God who delivered Israel from Egypt is the God who answers Hannah's prayer, who calls Isaiah, who walks into Galilee. Reading slowly, you see verses. Reading in sprints, you see Him.
  2. Hard books became readable. Leviticus, Numbers, Chronicles, Ezekiel — all the parts most people give up on — actually flow when you read them at speed. The rhythm of the law, the genealogies, the lists; they were never meant to be parsed line by line. They were meant to be read.
  3. The Bible got smaller. I don't mean smaller in importance. I mean smaller in scale. Reading the whole Bible used to feel like climbing a mountain. After my first sprint it felt like a long walk with a friend. Once you've done it, you know you can do it again.

What I'd say to you

You don't need a theology degree. You don't need a Hebrew dictionary. You don't need to be a "morning person" or a "discipline person" or a "Bible person". You need an open Bible, twenty minutes, and the willingness to keep going when it gets dry. Some chapters are dry. Read them anyway. The whole Bible is the gift; not just your favourite parts of it.

If you'd like to try, Bible Sprint is free. Pick a pace that fits your life — gentle (170 days at one sprint a day), brisk, or sprint (43 days at four sprints a day). It works on any phone or laptop. You can read or listen. Your journal stays private. Your progress is your own.

The Lord taught me this. Now I'm passing it on.
